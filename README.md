# Philosophical Foundations of Self-Sovereign Identity

The concept of identity goes beyond technology and is rooted in human nature 
in all its complexity. Addressing the topic of identity means first starting 
from a more complete and articulated reasoning about what the human being 
itself is, since the very fact that a topic so primordial to the human species 
is still the subject of heated debate today is proof that the questions from 
which it unfolds cannot be approached with a gaze merely introjected into 
the present.

## What is Identity?

The term Identity comes from the Latin *idem*, a demonstrative pronoun meaning 
"the same/the very same," and the suffix *-tas*, indicating "quality and 
condition." Here we already have a first element: identity is the "condition 
of the self," that which defines what a particular subject is and is not.

From Wikipedia: *"In relation to other objects, identity is everything that 
makes an entity definable and recognizable, because it possesses a set of 
qualities or characteristics that make it what it is and, for that very reason, 
distinguish it from all other entities."*

Identity therefore makes sense if:

- It is placed in relation to "other objects," to something that is other than 
what the identity represents;
- It possesses unique characteristics, which therefore distinguish it from 
other identities.

A clarification is necessary: identity belongs to the subject, that is, to the 
individual as the center of conscious experience. Identity is what makes the 
subject such — what makes the subject the "center" of its experience — since 
identity "defines" whoever holds it, that is, it establishes the *fines* — the 
boundaries — of the subject, and in doing so necessarily also determines its 
center.

## The State and Digital Identity

In the mainstream narrative, identity is a concept typically associated with 
very negative connotations. Yet this philosophical stance legitimizes another 
justification: if identity is dangerous, then it is desirable for the state to 
enter the dispute — perhaps by itself determining what can legitimately be 
"identity," or by making access to one's own identity contingent on the 
arbitrary will of the state itself.

This is precisely why the forms of Digital Identity that many states are 
preparing or have already made public are nothing more than a domesticating 
appropriation of individuals' identities.

**But why must sovereignty over identity necessarily be technical and 
decentralized? Could a democratic state not guarantee the same sovereignty?**

This question starts from the assumption that the state acts in good faith. 
But that is precisely the point: no one can guarantee that the state does 
"good" for its citizens. The modern state was born from the need to centralize 
and homogenize the particular demands of peripheral actors, and to do so it 
is indispensable to exercise absolute power over its geographical and political 
peripheries — regardless of how "democratic" a country may be.

Giving a state total control over the identities of its citizens means 
delegating the very faculty of existing as individual identities. Self-Sovereign 
Digital Identity exists precisely because there is a demand for decentralization 
arising from growing impatience and distrust toward democratic state 
institutions.

> This is not a pro-anarchist or anti-state position. The point is that the 
> state does as it pleases because it holds the monopoly on force. It is a 
> system with clear pros and cons — any centralized architecture simultaneously 
> presents advantages and disadvantages in having a single entity holding 
> control over the data of many users. This is not a matter of opinion.

The SSI system cannot be coercive. It is up to the individual to choose whether 
to prefer centralized systems over decentralized ones. My task is to raise a 
question and offer an alternative — not to convince the detractors of the 
decentralized system.

## Technical Foundations

Decentralization is **not** a dogmatic ideological position — it is the most 
technically efficient solution to structural problems of the traditional 
centralized system.

A system for Identity can be truly sovereign if the following technical 
conditions are met:

1. **No Single Point of Failure** — Control of cryptographic keys belongs 
exclusively to the subject. If keys are held by third parties, a subject cannot 
sign, cannot approve or deny the use of their own data.

2. **No Single Point of Revocation** — No entity can unilaterally revoke or 
modify the identity. Power must be fragmented.

3. **No Single Point of Surveillance** — Verifiability does not require a 
central online authority. Consensus must be distributed so that the interruption 
of one node does not affect the fungibility of the system.

4. **No Single Point of Control** — Access cannot be denied from a single point 
of control. Validation must be independent of the identity of the validator, so 
as to avert the risk of censorship and discrimination.

Until proven otherwise, decentralization is today the most efficient solution 
to these problems.

## Centralized vs Decentralized

A centralized system works if there is trust in the central authority. A 
decentralized system does not operate on trust, but on the absence of its own 
agenda — on the transparency and verifiability of the protocol, on its 
deterministic, mathematical operation divorced from any human decision driven 
by private interests.

If tomorrow CBDCs were to replace physical currency, a citizen would find 
themselves compelled to adopt that instrument with no ability to hold it 
accountable. Bitcoin, by contrast, will exist only if demand for BTC remains 
sustained over time. **Decentralized products survive and thrive only if there 
is genuine bottom-up demand. Centralized products can do without it.**

In a decentralized system, power is structurally limited: if this structure 
breaks down, the product dies, since popular consensus depends precisely on 
its immutability. In a centralized system, power is theoretically limited by 
laws — words on paper. In practice, power is autonomous and absolute, otherwise 
it could not exercise the executive functions for which it is in command.
